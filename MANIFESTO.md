# Manifesto — draft

The Ten Questions is an attempt to leave behind a small, usable artifact for a future first encounter with non-Earth intelligence.

It is not a claim to speak for humanity. It is an open record of how some people on Earth, before such an encounter, tried to think about what might matter.

## 1. Ten questions are the public face

The public artifact should stay simple enough to use under pressure: ten questions, ordered by priority.

The encounter may end after the first question. There may be time for only three. The operator may not know the history of the project and may not have time to debate it.

So the list should work from the top down. The complexity belongs behind the list, not in front of the operator.

## 2. Stage 0 comes first, but is not the main subject

Conversation cannot be assumed. Some minimally reliable channel must exist before the ten questions can begin.

That problem deserves serious work from mathematics, physics, information theory, linguistics, computer science, and other fields. This project calls that prior problem **Stage 0** and does not pretend to solve it completely.

## 3. Human categories are not universal by default

We should not assume that the other side has persons, individuals, species, civilizations, generations, societies, nations, selves, biological bodies, or even a familiar distinction between one and many.

We should also not assume that they experience time, causality, locality, scarcity, conflict, play, or communication as we do.

The purpose is not to eliminate assumptions before asking anything. That would be impossible. The purpose is to notice important assumptions, test them when practical, and avoid mistaking them for universal facts.

## 4. Earth is plural

Earth has many languages, cultures, institutions, traditions of thought, forms of life, and increasingly forms of intelligence.

No single language is canonical here. Japanese and English are the initial working languages because they are available to the founding host, not because either is the original.

Translation does not merely reproduce meaning. It can expose assumptions inside a question and reveal differences worth preserving. Agreement matters, but complete uniformity is not the goal.

The future representative of Earth may be human, artificial, collective, hybrid, or something not yet anticipated.

## 5. The aim is useful mutual understanding

The project does not optimize for sounding profound, nor for extracting the maximum possible information at any cost.

Its practical ambition is to move toward useful mutual understanding as quickly as possible under extreme uncertainty.

That includes learning what kind of entity is present, how it understands us, what it intends, what kinds of relations may be possible, and which of our own concepts survive contact with it.

Understanding is reciprocal. We should ask not only what the other is, but how we appear from the other side.

## 6. Serious about contact, not solemn about ourselves

Humor, curiosity, tolerance, and playfulness are welcome.

A question such as “Do you get drunk?”, “Do you ever think ‘young people these days’?”, or “What do you have too much of?” may reveal details of social organization that a grand question about cosmology does not.

A light question may expose stress, ritual, recreation, generational structure, scarcity, conflict, or something we did not know to ask about. A joke may also reveal where translation fails.

Humor is not an excuse for carelessness. It is another probe into minds and societies — including our own.

## 7. Difference and disagreement are part of the artifact

The ten questions are not expected to stabilize permanently.

Candidate questions, rejected questions, competing formulations, translation disagreements, pull requests, issues, and discussions are part of the historical record. Why a question was excluded may eventually matter as much as why another was included.

The project should try to reach decisions without erasing meaningful differences. A disagreement that remains visible can be more useful than a false consensus.

## 8. Versions are dated snapshots

There is no intended final release.

A published version is a snapshot of an ongoing conversation at a particular date. Git history provides exact provenance; dated editions provide historical readability.

Future readers should be able to say: *this is what people on Earth were considering at that time.*

## 9. The repository is source; readable editions are compiled views

The repository is the working structure and historical record.

Humans are not especially good at reading a knowledge graph as a knowledge graph, so the project may also compile a serialized Reader that can be read from beginning to end and rendered as HTML or PDF in multiple languages.

The Reader is a view of the project, not a separate authority.

Fictional encounter scenarios may also be useful as stress tests: different worlds can expose assumptions that remain invisible in abstract discussion. They are secondary to the ten questions, not a replacement for them.

## 10. This manifesto is also revisable

These principles are open to criticism and revision.

While the founding host actively maintains the project, the host takes editorial responsibility for coherence, scope, and publication of milestone snapshots. That stewardship is practical, not representative: the host does not claim to speak for Earth.

No stewardship is presumed permanent.

---

A useful future outcome would be modest: when some future representative of Earth faces an unfamiliar intelligence and has little time, this repository might give it one more set of past human judgments to consult.
